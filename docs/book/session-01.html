3.1.6 Optional, and worth it

ISLR ch. 2, free at https://www.statlearning.com/. Sections 2.1–2.2 give the vocabulary — \(f\), \(\varepsilon\), reducible and irreducible error — used from Session 02 onward.

the-supervised-learning-problem.md covers the same ground in forty minutes, with the derivations written out and five self-check questions: the model \(Y = f(X) + \varepsilon\), loss and risk, why \(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\) is the best predictor there is, and what the irreducible error \(\sigma^2\) means.

Neither is needed for Session 01, which has no mathematics in it, and neither is examinable. Session 02 introduces everything it needs.

3.4 1 · What this course is

3.4.1 The organising question

3.4.2 When can a number about the past be used to make a claim about the future?

Every method in the eleven sessions that follow is an answer to some version of that question — and every one of them comes with conditions.

  • Twelve sessions, each 90 minutes of teaching + 90 minutes of group work
  • Every method derived before it is used
  • Everything runs on your machine, with open tools
  • No API key, no subscription, no internet needed during a practice

3.4.3 What makes this course unusual

You are expected to work with a language model throughout. It is a required tool, not a tolerated one.

And the standing rule: every deliverable must document at least one instance where you identified an LLM output as wrong, unverifiable, or misleading — and explain how you established that.

You are never penalised for using the model. You are penalised for using it uncritically.

3.4.4 Why that rule is the point

Language models made information abundant. They did not make abundant the ability to ask the right question or to recognise a wrong answer. Both are functions of expertise.

A concrete case, from Session 05. Ask a model to select variables and it will run a lasso and hand you a list. The list looks authoritative. Only if you know the lasso is unstable under correlated predictors will you ask the follow-up that matters — “how often is each of these selected across bootstrap replicates?” — and turn a misleading answer into a useful one.

The model will not volunteer that question. You have to know enough to ask.

3.6 3 · How you are judged

3.6.1 The four evaluations

Component Weight Form
Participation 10% individual, continuous
Midterm exam 30% individual, on paper — pen and calculator only
Team work 20% groups of three, Session 12
Final exam 40% individual research paper (25) + individual oral, 15 min (15)

Two are individual and carry 70%. The group cannot carry you, and you cannot carry the group.

3.6.2 From a number to a letter

The four components give a mark out of 100. That mark is then converted to a letter grade, and the conversion is curved — with the class median at B+.

Within each component, nothing is curved. Participation is scored against the published absolute bar; the midterm and the paper are marked against their rubrics. The curve applies once, at the end, so an assessment that turned out harder than intended does not cost you a letter.

It is not a quota, and it is not a race against the person next to you.

3.6.3 Participation — 10%

Attendance is assumed, so participation cannot mean turning up. It means active engagement — and it is earned in three ways, any of which counts.

In the room a question that changed how another group saw their result, a connection between two angles, a correction that landed
In the practice working the ninety minutes: your own commits, the two-minute report when drawn, engaging with what other groups found
In the optional lab a completed QMIB Lab App knowledge check, done at home after a session

What is marked is sustained engagement across the term — by any mix of the three. Not a count of interventions, and never a ranking against each other.

3.6.4 The optional labs

After each session the QMIB Lab App offers an at-home knowledge check. It is never a prerequisite for the next lecture, and it is never marked for correctness.

It opens during the term — I will give you the link in class. Nothing is expected of you before then.

A completed report counts toward participation because attempting it is the engagement being measured. It exists so you can find out privately whether you actually followed the session — and so that full marks do not depend on speaking up in a room of thirty.

Failing to deliver the two-minute report when drawn is still the clearest participation failure there is.

3.6.5 Midterm — 30%

  • On paper. Pen, and a simple four-function calculator
  • No computer, no phone, no internet, no notes
  • Wednesday 28 October, two hours, covering Sessions 1–7
  • Working is marked: a correct answer with no derivation earns part marks

No machine, and no assistant. Every other assessment assumes a model beside you, because that is how the work is now done. This one deliberately does not.

It is the only instrument that isolates what you can derive unaided — which is the thing that lets you tell when the assistant is wrong, for the rest of your career.

3.6.6 Team work — 20%

Model governance file 7
Reproducible analysis 7
Revised Session 1 memo + change log 3
Presentation and defence 3

Eleven weeks with a real client: a student in the SDAfrique programme who identified the problem and will use what you produce. She is your client, not your co-author.

An individual multiplier from confidential peer ratings, gated on the git history, applies to this 20% only — never to the midterm, never to the final.

Default ×1.00. A mark is reduced only when two independent records agree.

3.6.7 Final exam — 40%

  • Research paper, written alone — 25
  • Individual oral, 15 minutes, no documents — 15

6,000–8,000 words, including references and appendices · a research question on an international topic · 10 academic articles in the literature review · the data · the model · the results.

The paper is a methods paper written for a real reader: an SDAfrique student in Benin who works in R, on a modest machine, with limited bandwidth, on a problem she chose herself.

A paper is written slowly, with help, over weeks. Fifteen minutes without documents establishes what you can reconstruct and defend in real time — which is what a referee or an employer actually tests.

3.7 4 · Working with a language model

3.7.1 The rule, as a publisher states it

This course applies the standard Elsevier and Emerald apply to submitted work.

  • AI may be used to improve readability and language — not to produce findings
  • Authors are fully responsible for the content, including anything the tool produced
  • Use must be disclosed — what tool, for what, where
  • AI cannot be an author. Authorship implies responsibility a tool cannot hold

It is a research assistant, not a co-author. It does not do the job; it helps you do the job.

3.7.2 What that means in practice

Ask it to argue against you. Its most useful output is an objection you had not considered.

  • Verify every factual claim — especially library APIs, statistical results and citations
  • It will state plausible falsehoods with complete confidence
  • Record what you checked, and how

Every deliverable carries an LLM statement: what you used, for what, and the one output you caught being wrong.

3.7.3 Ethics and integrity

HEC Montréal’s Règlement sur les fraudes et plagiat applies in full. Two clarifications specific to this course:

  • Model use is permitted and expected, subject to the documentation rule. Presenting model output as your own reasoning, without that verification, is misrepresentation
  • Code from any source may be used with attribution. Copy a Stack Overflow answer, cite it in a comment. Unattributed borrowing is plagiarism; attributed borrowing is normal practice

All data in this course are public. Respect each licence, record provenance, and check before redistributing anything.

3.8 5 · What I expect

3.8.1 Four expectations

  1. Arrive prepared. The paper read, the data loaded, the self-check attempted
  2. Say the units. A coefficient without units is not a finding
  3. State what your result does not license. Every deliverable in this course asks for it
  4. Push your own commits. Everyone in the group, every session. The log is the record

And one more, which is the hardest: a well-argued refusal earns full marks. “This cannot be claimed, and here is what would be needed” is the answer I am looking for.

3.8.2 What loses marks, everywhere

  • A number reported without its uncertainty
  • An association described in causal language
  • A model chosen because it gave the answer you wanted
  • An LLM output used without a verification you can point to
  • A result that does not reproduce from a clean clone

A result that does not reproduce is not a result. That is not pedantry — it is the standard every serious journal now applies.

3.9 In the practice

3.9.1 A conversation: Europe 2031, and what AI actually is

This is not a practice. There is no code, no deliverable and no submission folder. It is a discussion, and the only preparation is having read the scenario.

Open the practice slides · source: MATH60033A-S01-Practice.qmd

3.9.1.1 What you should have read

Europe 2031 — online, as a PDF, in French, or as audio. Then the critical brief, 00-pre-session/reading-europe-2031.md.

Bring your annotations. You will be asked what you underlined and why.

3.9.1.2 Part 1 — The scenario (~45 min)

3.9.1.2.1 Its architecture, first
Part Period Epistemic status
Prologue Jan 2025 – Jun 2026 Fact-based — the observed record
Scenario Aug 2026 – Mar 2031 Speculative — a constructed path
Epilogue Jun 2034 Speculative — a constructed endpoint

The seam between the first row and the second is the most interesting line in the document. We will find it together and ask what changes as you cross it.

3.9.1.2.2 The questions
  1. Where does the evidence stop and the story start? Point to a paragraph. Defend the choice.
  2. Which claims could be checked in 2027? Not “will it happen” — what observable would move your belief, and who publishes it?
  3. Whose scenario is it? Written by a foundation, with named authors, at a moment. What would a different author have made the protagonists worry about?
  4. What is conspicuously absent? A scenario is defined as much by what it does not imagine.
  5. If you believed it, what would you do differently on Monday? If nothing, what is it for?

A scenario is a conditional object. It says if these things, then those things. It is not a forecast and it does not carry a probability. Confusing the two is the first error this course exists to prevent — and the last, in Session 11, when you write your own governance file.

3.9.1.3 Part 2 — What AI actually is (~45 min)

You have just installed a model on your laptop. Before the course starts using that word for twelve weeks, we should agree what it denotes.

3.9.1.3.1 The questions
  1. What is the thing you downloaded? You pulled roughly four gigabytes. What is in the file?
  2. What is it doing when it answers? Not metaphorically. What computation runs?
  3. Where did its competence come from, and where does it stop? Why is it fluent about a library and unreliable about the version you have installed?
  4. Is the model in the Europe 2031 scenario the same kind of object as the one on your laptop? The scenario turns on capability. Whose capability, doing what?
  5. What in this course is “AI” and what is a hundred-year-old statistical method with a new name? Sessions 02 to 05 are older than the term. Does that matter?
3.9.1.3.2 The distinction to leave with

Prediction is not inference, and neither is understanding.

A model that predicts well may be useless for choosing a policy — that is Session 10. A model that fits the past perfectly may be worthless next year — that is Sessions 04 and 11. And a system fluent in a subject may hold no representation of it at all.

3.9.1.4 How the room works

  • Everyone speaks. Not everyone at length, but nobody silent for ninety minutes.
  • Positions are provisional. Change your mind out loud; that is the demonstration, not the embarrassment.
  • Cite the text. “Page 14 says” beats “I feel like”.
  • Disagree with me. Especially about what AI is. I hold views; they are not the answer key.

There is no right answer to any question above, and no mark attached to today. Participation is recorded from Session 02, when the practice sessions begin.

Session 02 begins the mathematics — least squares, as a right-angle triangle — and the practice sessions start with your project and your group.

Before then, complete the Session 02 setup guide: git, a GitHub account, and your group’s private repository. Budget 45 minutes.
