## 1. What it is — and what it is not

*Europe 2031* is an **independent scenario narrative** published on **11 June 2026**. It is **not**
an official European Union forecast.

Its architecture matters as much as its content:

| Part | Period | Epistemic status |
|---|---|---|
| Prologue | January 2025 – June 2026 | **Fact-based** — drawn from the observed record |
| Main narrative | August 2026 – March 2031 | **Speculative** — a constructed path |
| Epilogue | June 2034 | **Speculative** — a constructed endpoint |

A reader who does not track the seam between the first row and the second will mistake a story for
evidence. Locating that seam is the first analytical skill this course asks of you.

---

## 2. How to use it

Treat *Europe 2031* as a **stress test**: a deliberately coherent adverse path whose purpose is to
help decision-makers expose assumptions, dependencies and missing capabilities.

Two rules follow directly.

1. **Do not cite its future events or numbers as forecasts.** They are internal parameters of a
   constructed narrative.
2. **Compare it with other exploratory work.** The European Commission's Joint Research Centre
   publishes four reference foresight scenarios for 2040 which are also *exploratory* rather than
   *predictive*. Reading the two side by side reveals how much of any scenario is method and how
   much is authorial choice.

> **Why a methods course opens with a narrative.** Every model you will build this term is a
> scenario: a story about how the world generates data, with the story's assumptions written as
> equations. The virtue of a good model is not that its assumptions are true — they never are — but
> that they are *stated*, so that someone can disagree with them precisely. Applying that standard
> to a piece of prose is the best possible warm-up for applying it to a regression.

---

## 3. The core scenario logic

The narrative advances through six linked stages. Read each row as a *claim*, and ask what evidence
would support or undermine it.

| Stage | Scenario claim |
|---|---|
| **Starting condition** | Europe lags the United States and China in frontier AI, compute and platform scale, while remaining economically dependent on both. |
| **Economic shock** | Automation and labour displacement weaken wage growth, tax revenues and the fiscal basis of European welfare states. |
| **Industrial pressure** | Chinese manufacturing and robotics strength compress European industry; dependence on US models and cloud limits autonomy. |
| **Political effect** | Member-state interests diverge, complicating collective investment and bargaining. |
| **Strategic leverage** | ASML, industrial capabilities, market access and alliances still give Europe bargaining power — but only if coordinated. |
| **End-state dilemma** | Europe faces a poor choice between deeper US dependence, accommodation with China, or an underpowered attempt at autonomy. |

Notice the structure: each stage is a **conditional consequence** of the one above it. The narrative
is therefore only as strong as its weakest link — which is precisely what a stress test invites you
to find.

## 4. Illustrative scenario parameters

The fictional 2031 scenario assigns:

| Quantity (2031) | United States | Europe | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI compute stock | 255.1 GW | 20.9 GW | ≈ 12.2× |
| Annual buildout | 29.3 GW | 1.9 GW | ≈ 15.7× |

> **⚠️ These figures are internal parameters of the story, not measured forecasts.**

Treat them the way you would treat the coefficients in a calibrated simulation model: they define
the world the narrative inhabits, and the interesting question is not *"are they right?"* but
*"what has to be true for them to be the right order of magnitude, and what would the story look
like if they were half as large?"*

Note also what the choice of *unit* does. Measuring capability in **gigawatts** embeds a claim —
that electrical power is the binding constraint on AI capability. That is an assumption, and it is
contestable. A different unit (accelerator-hours, trained-model quality, deployed applications per
worker) would tell a different story with the same underlying world.

---

## 5. Policy responses embedded in the narrative

The scenario does not only diagnose; it prescribes. Its policy menu:

- **Build capacity.** European compute, chips, data centres and power capacity.
- **Special Compute Zones.** Compress permitting, assemble concierge teams, and reuse grid
  connections from retired power plants.
- **Governed interdependence.** Work with US hyperscalers while anchoring critical infrastructure
  and governance under European law.
- **A coalition of capable middle powers.** Canada, Japan and Korea, together with France, Germany,
  the Netherlands, Norway, the United Kingdom and EU institutions.
- **Industrial acceleration.** Robotics and industrial AI, usable data, and targeted investment
  screening.
- **A renewed flexicurity.** Retraining combined with wage insurance, mobility support and employer
  flexibility.
- **Political vision and safety capacity.** A positive narrative, plus credible capacity for
  international safety dialogue.

**Analytical exercise while reading:** for each item, ask which of the six stages in section 3 it
targets. Some target several; at least one targets none. A policy menu that does not map onto the
causal chain it claims to address is a common failure mode in strategy documents, and it is easy to
spot once you look for it.

## 6. Critical reading — the table you will use in the lab

This is the heart of the pre-session work. Each row states a premise of the narrative and the
question a decision-maker should put to it.

| Type | *Europe 2031* premise | Question for decision-makers |
|---|---|---|
| **Assumption A** | Very rapid AI capability progress and diffusion. | Test slower progress, bottlenecks, regulation and sector variation. |
| **Assumption B** | Compute becomes the dominant measure of geopolitical power. | Add energy, data, talent, organisational capability, industrial integration and legitimacy. |
| **Assumption C** | Europe responds slowly and remains fragmented. | Model earlier coordinated investment or stronger member-state coalitions. |
| **Assumption D** | US–China bloc dynamics dominate. | Test India, Gulf states, Japan, Korea, Canada and issue-specific coalitions. |
| **Value** | Makes interdependence and social-contract risks vivid. | Use it to design indicators, trigger points and no-regret actions. |

**In the lab, each group is assigned one row.** Your task will be to convert the premise into
something a statistician could actually test — an observable indicator, a direction, a trigger
point and a falsifier. Come having thought about which row you would want.

## 7. Discussion questions

These are the questions the class will open with. Prepare a position on at least three.

1. Which scenario assumption would you change first, and how would that alter Europe's choices?
2. Is **"compute sovereignty"** feasible, or is **governed interdependence** a better objective?
3. How should firms split investment among frontier models, industrial AI, workforce redesign and
   resilience?
4. What fiscal and labour-market policies preserve both adaptability and social protection?
5. What roles can **Canada** and **Japan** play that neither the United States nor China can fill?
6. How can safety, multilingualism and multicultural legitimacy become sources of **productivity**
   rather than compliance overhead?

## 9. A note on sources and honesty

This brief summarises a scenario document. Where it states numbers (255.1 GW, 20.9 GW, 29.3 GW,
1.9 GW, and the ratios 12.2 and 15.7), those numbers are **reproduced from the scenario**, not
verified against any external measurement — because there is nothing to verify them against: they
describe 2031.

Adopt the same discipline in your own work. When you report a number, be able to say in one sentence
where it came from and what would falsify it. Sessions 3, 4 and 12 return to this repeatedly; it is
the through-line of the course.
